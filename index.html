Occupational Therapy · Driver Assessment

Fitness-to-Drive Screening Matrix

A structured, evidence-informed off-road screen that estimates the likelihood a client will pass an on-road occupational therapy driving assessment — built from published predictive-validity data, not intuition.

This is a decision-support aid, not a substitute for clinical judgement or a licensing decision. No off-road battery has been shown to fully replace an on-road assessment. Published tools such as DriveSafe/DriveAware only classify roughly half of clients confidently as "likely safe" or "likely unsafe" off-road; the remainder still require full on-road testing. Use this matrix to structure your reasoning and triage referrals — always integrate it with clinical interview, medical/functional history, collateral report, and (where indicated) an on-road evaluation before any recommendation about driving cessation, restriction, or continuation.
1

Score the domains

Six domains, weighted by the strength of their published predictive validity for on-road pass/fail outcomes. Select the option that best reflects the client's off-road performance in each.

Cognitive screen (MoCA / MMSE)

weight ×1.0

MMSE score was one of four subtests retained in the OT‑DORA classification tree predicting on-road fitness-to-drive after stroke (Unsworth et al., AUC 0.83).

Trail Making Test — Part B

weight ×1.5

The "3-or-3 rule" (≥3 min or ≥3 errors) has the most consistent support across 47 reviewed studies; a stricter ≥3.58 min cutoff in cognitively-impaired clients referred for full evaluation showed 74% sensitivity / 62% specificity (Krasniuk et al., 2023).

Useful Field of View (subtest 2/3)

weight ×1.5

UFOV subtest 3 (selective attention) outperformed Trails B in a comprehensive-evaluation sample: AUC 0.76 vs 0.70, with a ≥467ms cutoff giving 79% sensitivity / 74% specificity for on-road pass/fail (Krasniuk et al., 2023).

DriveSafe / DriveAware

weight ×3.0 — strongest predictor

Highest predictive validity of any office-based tool reviewed: trichotomised classification correctly identifies ~90%+ of the ~50% of clients it confidently sorts as "safe" or "unsafe" (Kay, Bundy & Cheal, 2009; Vito et al. systematic review, 2023). The remaining clients require full on-road testing regardless of score.

Self-awareness of driving ability

weight ×2.0

Awareness of one's own driving performance is the construct DriveAware was built to isolate, and reduced insight (anosognosia) is repeatedly flagged in the OT fitness-to-drive literature as a marker of elevated on-road risk independent of raw skill scores.

Physical / functional screen

weight ×1.0

One physical subtest (e.g. Right Heel Pivot Test, rapid pace walk, cervical/trunk ROM) was retained alongside the three cognitive subtests in the validated OT‑DORA prediction tree (Unsworth et al., 2018).

Score every domain for the most accurate output — unscored domains are treated as missing, not zero.

Diagnosis-specific red flags

The matrix above screens function, not diagnosis — but knowing which brain region or system a condition affects tells you what to watch for during the off-road session and what the on-road assessor should specifically probe. Grouped below by aetiology, each entry names the region/mechanism, the red flags to watch for, and links to the relevant Austroads standard.

All diagnosis-specific medical standards for Australian and New Zealand licensing are set out in Austroads' Assessing Fitness to Drive (AFTD). Functional red flags below support your off-road reasoning; the licensing standard itself is the authority on reporting obligations and conditional-licence criteria.
Cerebrovascular
Stroke — right hemisphere / parietal hemispatial neglect
  • Left-sided neglect present in up to ~80% of acute right-hemisphere strokes — watch for missed hazards, pedestrians, or signage on the left
  • Collisions with left-side obstacles (door frames, kerbs, parked cars) during mobility or transfers
  • Under-scanning to the left at intersections and during lane changes
  • Reduced awareness or denial of the deficit (anosognosia) — self-report of safety is unreliable
  • Screen with cancellation/line bisection tasks; a clear screen does not rule out neglect that only appears in a cluttered, dynamic scene like traffic
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Stroke — frontal lobe executive / behavioural
  • Impulsivity and disinhibition — risky overtaking, reduced gap judgement
  • Poor hazard anticipation and slowed decision-making at intersections
  • Perseveration — continuing an action (accelerating, a turn sequence) past the point it's appropriate
  • Reduced self-monitoring and insight, often with intact basic motor and visual skills, so bedside exam alone can look reassuring
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Stroke — left hemisphere / language-dominant aphasia, apraxia
  • Comprehension deficits affecting understanding of road signs or verbal/written test instructions — clarify true cognitive status isn't obscured by a language barrier
  • Apraxia disrupting sequenced motor actions such as indicating, checking mirrors, and changing gear in order
  • Right-sided weakness or sensory loss affecting steering and pedal control
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Stroke — posterior / occipital (PCA territory) visual field loss
  • Homonymous hemianopia — missed hazards in the blind hemifield, especially peripheral
  • Some clients compensate well with head/eye scanning; others show little awareness of the field loss
  • Confirm with formal visual field testing rather than confrontation testing alone before assuming adequate compensation
Austroads: vision and eye disorders →
Traumatic
Traumatic brain injury diffuse / frontotemporal-predominant
  • Presentation varies widely with injury pattern and severity — avoid assuming a uniform profile
  • Common: slowed processing speed, impaired divided attention, impulsivity
  • Fatigue-related decline over the course of a single drive or assessment — a good start can mask late deterioration
  • Overconfidence relative to actual ability is common and can be more dangerous than the underlying deficit itself
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Neurodegenerative
Alzheimer's disease medial temporal / temporoparietal
  • Short-term memory loss — getting lost on previously familiar routes
  • Difficulty adapting to novel or complex traffic situations more than routine ones
  • Insight is often relatively preserved early, then declines — reassessment interval matters more than a single screen
Austroads: dementia →
Vascular dementia diffuse subcortical / patchy
  • Stepwise rather than smooth decline — function can drop sharply after a new vascular event
  • Prominent slowed processing speed and executive dysfunction relative to memory
  • Presentation depends heavily on lesion location, so functional testing matters more than the diagnosis label alone
Austroads: dementia →
Frontotemporal dementia frontal / temporal
  • Early personality change, impulsivity, and disinhibition — often before memory is noticeably affected
  • Reduced insight typically precedes obvious cognitive decline — self- and family-report may both underestimate risk
  • Flag as elevated risk for continued unsafe driving despite the client's subjective confidence
Austroads: dementia →
Dementia with Lewy bodies occipital / subcortical, fluctuating
  • Prominent visuospatial impairment and visual hallucinations
  • Fluctuating cognition and alertness — a single good screening session may not represent typical function
  • Parkinsonian motor signs (see Parkinson's disease below) frequently co-occur
Austroads: dementia →
Mild cognitive impairment variable, often amnestic
  • Many clients continue to drive safely — MCI alone is not a reason to stop, but does warrant monitoring
  • Amnestic MCI carries an elevated risk of progression, so build in a shorter re-assessment interval
Austroads: dementia →
Movement disorders
Parkinson's disease basal ganglia
  • Bradykinesia and slowed reaction time, particularly for emergency braking
  • Rigidity and tremor affecting fine steering and pedal control
  • Visuospatial deficits independent of motor symptoms
  • Motor fluctuations ("on/off" periods) — time the assessment to reflect a typical, not best-case, medication state
  • Excessive daytime sleepiness or sudden-onset sleep episodes from dopaminergic medication
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Huntington's disease basal ganglia / striatal, progressive
  • Chorea (involuntary movement) directly interfering with steering and pedal control
  • Executive dysfunction and impulsivity
  • Progressive psychiatric symptoms can compound risk beyond the motor presentation
  • Progressive condition — plan for shortening re-assessment intervals over time rather than a one-off clearance
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Neurodevelopmental & congenital
Down syndrome global / diffuse
  • Variable intellectual disability affecting rule learning and complex, time-pressured decision-making
  • Generally slower reaction time — comprehensive individualised functional assessment is essential rather than a diagnosis-based exclusion
  • Elevated risk of early-onset Alzheimer's disease from the 40s — build earlier and more frequent cognitive re-screening into follow-up
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Autism spectrum variable, individual
  • No uniform driving profile — presentation and driving risk vary greatly between individuals; avoid assuming risk from the diagnosis alone
  • Where relevant: difficulty with rapid, multi-step decisions under time pressure, or sensory overload in busy/unpredictable traffic environments
  • Rigid rule-following can be a genuine safety strength rather than a deficit — assess function, not stereotype
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
ADHD frontostriatal
  • Inattention, impulsivity, and a documented association with elevated risk-taking behind the wheel
  • Medication timing and coverage across the length of the driving task is often the practical issue, not baseline ability
Austroads: psychiatric conditions →
Cerebral palsy motor / corticospinal
  • Spasticity, dystonia, or coordination difficulty affecting steering and pedal use — cognition is frequently unaffected
  • Vehicle modification and physical/functional assessment is usually the central issue rather than cognitive risk
Austroads: musculoskeletal conditions →
Demyelinating & neuromuscular
Multiple sclerosis white matter, variable location
  • Fatigue-related decline over the course of a drive — screen at a representative point in the client's typical fatigue cycle, not just first thing
  • Processing speed deficits and visual disturbance (optic neuritis)
  • Relapsing-remitting course means fitness can change meaningfully between scheduled reviews
Austroads: other neurological conditions →
Spinal cord injury motor / sensory, level-dependent
  • Motor and sensory function below the injury level — focus assessment on adaptive equipment and physical capacity
  • Cognition is typically intact unless there is a concurrent traumatic brain injury from the same incident — confirm this rather than assume it
Austroads: musculoskeletal conditions →
Seizure & episodic
Epilepsy / seizure disorders focal or generalised
  • The core risk is sudden loss of control, not an ongoing skills deficit — this off-road matrix is not the right tool to establish fitness here
  • Mandated seizure-free periods apply and are set by the licensing authority, not by off-road cognitive/motor performance
Austroads: seizures and epilepsy →
Cardiac syncope / arrhythmia cardiovascular
  • Risk of sudden incapacitation at the wheel, independent of cognitive or motor screening results
  • Cardiology clearance is the relevant gate here, not an off-road OT battery
Austroads: cardiovascular conditions →
Systemic / medical
Diabetes mellitus metabolic / peripheral / retinal
  • Hypoglycaemia unawareness — sudden incapacitation risk, ask specifically about awareness of low blood sugar episodes
  • Peripheral neuropathy affecting pedal sensation and fine control
  • Diabetic retinopathy affecting visual acuity and visual fields
Austroads: diabetes mellitus →
Psychiatric
Severe psychiatric illness acute psychosis, severe mood episode
  • Impaired judgement and insight during acute episodes — risk is often episodic rather than a stable trait
  • Sedating medication effects can add a separate, independent risk on top of the underlying illness
  • Reassess at illness stage transitions rather than relying on a single point-in-time clearance
Austroads: psychiatric conditions →
Sleep
Obstructive sleep apnoea & other sleep disorders excessive daytime sleepiness
  • Microsleep risk, particularly on monotonous or highway driving
  • Subjective sleepiness ratings correlate poorly with objective risk — don't rely on self-report alone
Austroads: sleep disorders →

When this evidence matters for an NDIS report

Under Section 34 of the NDIS Act, a support must meet all six "reasonable and necessary" criteria before it can be funded. Citing peer-reviewed evidence in your report is most useful where it directly helps establish one of these:

  1. Linked to the participant's goals — evidence that a strategy or support is effective for the participant's stated goals strengthens the link between the support and their plan.
  2. Supports social and economic participation — research demonstrating functional or participation gains (e.g. return to driving, community access, employment) helps justify this criterion.
  3. Value for money — evidence comparing outcomes or cost-effectiveness of an intervention can support value-for-money arguments, especially for higher-cost supports (e.g. equipment, intensive therapy blocks).
  4. Effective and evidence-based ("current good practice") — this is where citing validated assessment tools or intervention research is most directly relevant; the NDIA is more likely to fund supports with a demonstrated evidence base.
  5. Accounts for informal and other supports — evidence on the limits of family/carer-provided support (e.g. supervision burden research) can help justify formal funded supports.
  6. Most appropriately funded by the NDIS — evidence clarifying that a support falls within the NDIS's remit (rather than health, education, or another system) helps address this criterion.

In practice, criterion 4 (evidence-based / current good practice) is where most cited research earns its place — but framing your evidence explicitly against whichever criterion the NDIA is most likely to query strengthens the report. This is general information, not legal advice — refer to the NDIS Act and current NDIS Guidelines for the authoritative wording.
